Room Sentinel

Knows when your room is losing heat — and tells you why, where and what to do. Five temperature probes stream into a cloud backend that learns how the room normally behaves. When it cools faster than it should, Room Sentinel names the cause (an open window, a heater that stopped, a dead sensor), shows where it's coming from on a live 3D heat map, and pushes a forecast and a fix to your phone.

Problem & Context

A thermostat only knows one number: the temperature right now. It can't tell a room that's cooling because it's cold outside from one that's cooling because someone left a window open, and it says nothing until the room is already cold. Room Sentinel watches five points in the room instead of one, learns how that particular room normally gains and loses heat, and speaks up as soon as the room stops behaving the way the physics says it should.

Built in a weekend at StormHacks 2026 by a team of four, end to end: a touchscreen controller and Wi-Fi bridge on real hardware, a cloud backend and time-series database, a 3D website, and an iOS and Android app. I owned the detection pipeline and the room simulator, the 3D website, and the mobile app.

What It Does
  • 01Learns the room. A per-device thermal model, expected rate = g·(outside − room) + h·(heater − room) + c, re-fits how leaky the room is (g) and how strongly the heater warms it (h) from the last few hours of data.
  • 02Finds the cause and the side. When the room keeps cooling faster than the model can explain, it flags it, and the probe whose gap to the room centre grows fastest shows where the cold is coming from: the window or the door.
  • 03Catches what a thermostat can't. Heating that stopped, a failed probe (the DS18B20's −127 and 85 °C error values, impossible readings), and a device that went silent. A hand on a probe is recognised and ignored instead of raising an alarm.
  • 04Advice, not alarms. Each problem becomes one tracked issue (opened, escalated, reminded, resolved) with a plain-language fix and a forecast like "below 18 °C in about 25 min", so you get one useful alert instead of one every second.
  • 05Live 3D heat map. The website colours a 3D model of the room (and a 2D plan) from the five readings, with heatmap, airflow and haze layers and camera presets for each probe.
  • 06Mobile app with QR pairing. An Expo app for iOS and Android: the device shows a QR code, you scan it and confirm, and alerts arrive as push notifications.
  • 07Physics-based demo mode. A simulated room where you change the outside temperature, open the window or switch off the heater. It runs through exactly the same detector as real hardware, so anyone can try the live demo without the device.
Architecture

Device. Five DS18B20 probes feed an STM32F407 running FreeRTOS with an ILI9488 touchscreen. It passes readings as JSON over UART to an ESP32 (ESP-IDF), which handles Wi-Fi provisioning, HTTPS pairing, and a TLS WebSocket to the cloud. The device has no clock, so the server timestamps every frame on arrival and acknowledges it.

Cloud. A FastAPI backend (Python 3.12, SQLAlchemy and Alembic) runs in Docker on Render, pinned to a single always-on instance because live device sockets are held in process memory. Readings land in TimescaleDB hypertables on Tiger Cloud, and Grafana Cloud provides dashboards and alert rules. The website (three.js and GSAP as plain JavaScript modules with no build step) is hosted on Vercel, and the mobile app gets alerts through Expo Push.

The pipeline I built. Every reading, real or simulated, goes through the same five stages: collect (validate and timestamp), analyze (sensor health, device silence, the thermal model), advise (turn each finding into a recommendation), track (one issue per problem across its lifecycle), and notify (push to the owner's phones and update the live view). Because the simulator feeds the same detector, the team could develop and test detection without a physical room, and the public demo runs on it too.
